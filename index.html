      Chapter 1

      How a brief works

      Marketing owns the problem. Creative owns the answer, at the level that was asked for. The brief is where the two meet.

      If you read one thingAgree on the problem before anyone works on the answer.

      1.1

      What a brief is for

      LPA training

      The brief is the problem. Everything after it is an answer.

      If two people read the brief and come away with two different problems, it needs another pass.

      1.2

      Get / To / By

      Proposed

      The whole brief in three lines. If they're hard to write, the brief probably needs a little more focus.

      Write these before anything else. Every field after this backs up one of the three lines.

      Get

      Who you're talking to, and what they do or believe today.

      Ask: who exactly, and what's in the way?
      To

      What you want them to do or believe instead.

      Ask: what changes in their head, not just their cart?
      By

      The one message that moves them. It's the strategy, not the creative idea.

      Ask: what would make them believe it?

      Example

      Getbusy dinner deciders who haven't tried Meal SimpleTosee Meal Simple as a real dinner, not a shortcutByshowing it makes the 5 o'clock decision for them

      Meal Simple is H-E-B's line of ready-to-cook meals. It's the brief the training worked through, and the example used across this site.

      1.3

      The level of the ask

      Proposed

      Say which level creative is being asked for.

      PlatformThe one big idea everything else is checked against.
      ActivationHow the platform shows up in a channel or a moment.
      ExecutionThe specific asset: format, size, shot.

      If the idea is already decided (a mascot, a bracket), call it an execution brief. Try not to use "idea" and "concept" as separate levels. Most of the industry uses them to mean the same thing.

      1.4

      The weekly rhythm

      LPA training

      Briefs go out by Friday at noon. Kickoff is the following Thursday.

      There's no separate discovery meeting, so the brief does that job.

      1.5

      One person, plus who's overhearing

      Proposed, from an H-E-B brief

      Write to one person you can picture. Everyone else the media reaches is overhearing.

      The work should make sense to them, but it isn't written for them. A recent H-E-B brief put it this way: talk to the insiders, and let everyone else overhear.

      Broad reach can be right for media. The creative target is still one person.

      1.6

      The brief is the referee

      Proposed

      Every comment in review points back to a line in the brief.

      If it can't, it's probably taste rather than feedback.

      1.7

      If the strategy changes

      LPA training

      The brief gets rewritten and creative development restarts. Changing the strategy in a comment round is how work goes sideways.

      Chapter 3

      Insight builder

      An insight is a truth, the motivation behind it, and the tension it creates. Shoppers won't hand you one. You build it by watching what they do and asking why.

      If you read one thingA fact is what shoppers do. An insight is why it's hard for them.

      3.1

      The formula

      LPA training

      Every insight has three parts. Most insights that fall flat stop at the first one.

      Truth

      What the shopper believes, wants or feels.

      Ask: what do they do, or say they want?
      Motivation

      The real reason why it matters to them.

      Ask: why? Then ask again.
      Tension

      The contradiction or problem they can't solve on their own.

      Ask: where does that motivation get in their way?
      3.2

      The test

      Proposed rule, LPA examples

      A tension on its own is just a problem. A strong insight goes one step further: the thing they want is what causes the problem.

      That's what makes it feel true to the shopper, and it gives creative something real to solve.

      1. Read the motivation.What do they want, and why?
      2. Read the tension.What problem are they stuck with?
      3. Take the motivation away.If the problem goes away too, the motivation causes the tension. It passes.

        Chapter 4

        Objectives

        Three levels, each with a number and a date. Perception moves marketing, and marketing moves the business. The number gives magnitude, and magnitude gives creative direction.

        Three levels

        LPA training

          Chapter 7

          Giving feedback

          Separate the idea from the execution. Check the work against the brief, and trust your gut.

          If you read one thingGive feedback as objectives, not directives. Say what needs to change, not how to change it.

          7.1

          Ears, gut, head

          LPA training

          Listen first, react second, analyze third. Then speak.

          Ears

          Is the idea clear? What does the agency recommend?

          Just listen for now. Fixing comes later.
          Gut

          Write down your first reaction and one line on why.

          Analysis makes you forget it, and a gut reaction is how shoppers see the work. Watching video? Write it down after the first play, before anyone replays it.
          Head

          Does it deliver the brief?

          Then check it against CARDS (7.2).
          7.2

          CARDS

          LPA training

          Five checks for the head step. They help you explain your gut, not replace it.

          CConnectsOpens the mind or the heart of the target. Not you, them.
          AAmplifiableThe idea can travel across channels and keep going.
          RRecognizablePeople know it's us. A good idea with weak branding is fixed in execution, not thrown out.
          DDistinctiveStands apart from the category. Not so different it stops looking like H-E-B.
          SSimpleGets through in a split second.
          7.3

          Objectives, not directives

          LPA training

          Say what you want the work to accomplish, not how to do it.

          Comments that helpClear point of view, tied to the brief. Short. Honest. If you don't love it, it's okay to say so.
          Comments that missToo many, not ranked, or vague. Buzzwords. Personal taste with no reason. "Frankenstein" requests that bolt options together.

          Make statements, not questions. A question makes the team guess what you think.

          Leave visual solutions to the creative team. It's fine to ask whether one element could come across from another option.

          7.4

          In the meeting

          LPA training
          1. Start with the option you prefer and say why.
          2. Keep it to three improvements, the ones that matter most.
          3. Skip options you don't want to move forward, unless the agency recommended one. Then say why you disagree.
          4. If someone already made your point, just say "I agree" and move on.
          5. Early rounds are about the idea. Save execution notes for later rounds.

          Speak to an option you didn't pick when: the agency preferred it, you're the A or a C and see it differently from what was said, or it has something you want carried into the chosen idea.

          When the work misses: be clear and kind, and skip the compliment sandwich. Check whether the brief or the context changed. Bring a copy of the brief.

          Show your real reaction in the room. Share concerns in the room, not in a list afterward.

          7.5

          Who speaks, and when

          LPA training

          Roles come from the RACI: Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed. See 2.16, or the glossary.

          • The R goes first. That's the responsible person, usually the most junior commenter. Up to five minutes to organize thoughts, not to decide what they think.
          • Cs build on it. Give input, and still state a view and a recommendation. If the agency says "both," ask which one they'd pick.
          • The A listens to all of it, settles conflicts between Cs, and gives clear direction. LPA recommends the A attend the first creative presentation to avoid extra meetings and "telephone."
          • The A can hand off to the R after the first stage.
          • Keep the room small. Cs can leave once they've given feedback.
          • Summarize after. Ideally the creative side writes the recap and the A confirms it, so both sides agree on what was said.
          Open at H-E-B. There's no written rule for who the A is by project type, and the A often doesn't see the work until a later round. The training group's next step: name the RACI at kickoff and pilot the A in the first presentation on one large project. See the RACI field (2.16) and the checklist (6.1).

          LPA training
          MethodHow it worksUse it when
          DirectCreatives, R, Cs and A in one room. A spokesperson sums up where people disagree.The default. LPA calls it the ideal.
          HuddlePresentation, a huddle in another room, then feedback from one spokesperson. Roughly 20 minutes each, timing not confirmed.Many commenters, junior commenters, or many options.

          7.7

          Production counts

          LPA training

          Talent cost and shoot timing are valid creative feedback. Say them early, when they can still shape the idea.

          7.8

          When the strategy shifts

          LPA training

          If the strategy changes, pause the feedback and update the brief first.

          Big shiftRewrite the brief and re-brief the team. Then leaders decide the timing.
          Small changeCheck it against the brief before it goes into a comment round.
          Keeps happeningUsually it's people outside the RACI weighing in late. That's a sign to fix the brief process, not the work. See 1.7.

          How to use it

          Chapter 10

          Glossary

          Every term and acronym used on this site, in plain words.

          10.1

          Terms

          Proposed definitions
          A, R, C, I
          The four RACI roles. Accountable makes the call. Responsible does the work and speaks first in feedback. Consulted gives input. Informed is kept in the loop.
          Activation
          How a platform idea shows up in one channel or moment. See 1.3.
          CARDS
          Five checks for creative work: Connects, Amplifiable, Recognizable, Distinctive, Simple. See 7.2.
          Concept review
          The meeting where creative shows its first ideas. The studio hold gets sized here.
          Desired behavior
          What you want the shopper to do or believe after seeing the work. A shift in belief, not a sales number. See 2.2.
          Execution
          The specific asset: format, size, shot. See 1.3.
          Get / To / By
          The brief in three lines: who, what you want them to do or believe, and the message that moves them. See 1.2.
          Insight
          A truth, the motivation behind it, and the tension it creates. See chapter 3.
          Kickoff
          The meeting where the brief is presented to creative. Thursdays, the week after the brief goes out.
          LPA
          The firm that ran the two-day brief and feedback training this site is built on.
          Mandatories
          Things the work must include, like legal lines or a logo. Keep it to true requirements.
          Media target
          Who the media plan reaches. It's often wider than the strategic target.
          MSM
          The marketing manager who writes and owns the brief.
          OOH
          Out-of-home: billboards, transit and other ads people see outside.
          Platform
          The one big idea everything else is checked against. See 1.3.
          Pre-pro
          The pre-production meeting before a shoot, where the shot list, props and styling get locked.
          RACI
          The chart of who's Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed on a project. See 2.16.
          Reason to believe
          A fact that proves the benefit. Every one has to support the same benefit.
          Strategic target
          The one person the brief is written to, and their mindset. See 2.6.
          Studio hold
          Time held on the studio calendar at kickoff. Sized at concept review, confirmed or released at approval. See 5.4.
          Work types
          Marquee, Extended, Standard, Express, Revision and Exact pickup. Each sets how many weeks the work needs. See 5.2.
